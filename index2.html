Harish-Chandra

The boy who walked from physics into the deepest corners of mathematics.

Born in Uttar Pradesh in 1923, he became the chief engineer of harmonic analysis on semisimple Lie groups, a theory that grew into a basic tool of modern number theory. This page tells his story through data, a timeline, his own words and the ideas he left behind.

The story of a name

Why “Harish-Chandra” has a hyphen.

A king of truth

In his youth he was simply Harishchandra, named after a legendary king famous for truthfulness since the days of the Mahabharata.

A copy editor’s hyphen

The hyphen was added by the copy editor of his first scientific papers. He kept it, and later used “Chandra” as a family name for his daughters.

A warrior’s code

His family were Kshatriya, the warrior caste. Choosing an academic career was exceptional for his background.

His work, in plain language

Symmetry is everywhere. He studied its deepest structure.

Fourier analysis

Any wave on a line can be split into pure waves. The group involved is just the real numbers, and the Plancherel theorem says no energy is lost in the split.

His generalisation

Do the same for a non-commutative group such as a semisimple Lie group. The pure waves become irreducible representations, and finding the Plancherel formula became his life’s goal.

The enveloping algebra

His 1951 paper gave a construction of semisimple Lie algebras and the Harish-Chandra isomorphism. It won the Cole Prize.

Characters

He extended the “character” of a representation to infinite dimensions, where it becomes a distribution rather than a function.

Subquotient theorem

Every irreducible representation sits inside an explicit family called the principal series.

Discrete series

Representations that appear as discrete pieces of the whole space. Constructing them was the hardest part of his programme.

Philosophy of cusp forms

Understand the discrete part, and everything else can be built from it. It links his work to automorphic forms and number theory.

p-adic groups

From 1968 he built a parallel theory over p-adic numbers, which he called the Lefschetz principle.

Why it matters today

A note for the AI and data-science generation.

Symmetry runs modern science

Group symmetry is at the heart of modern physics. In machine learning, equivariant networks are built around symmetry groups, using the same mathematical language.

Fourier ideas

Harmonic analysis generalises the Fourier transform, an idea every signal-processing student already uses.

Pure maths pays off

Automorphic forms, which his work feeds into, are now central to analytic number theory.

A library book changed his path.

At Allahabad he found Dirac’s Principles of Quantum Mechanics. Its style and ideas made him want to be a physicist.

He found errors in André Weil’s book.

Within weeks of starting mathematics he read Weil’s book on integration on groups and reported several mistakes, including one in the proof of Pontrjagin duality. Weil fixed them in later editions.

“I am not interested in proofs.”

Dirac’s reply to his worries about rigour pushed him to leave physics. He felt he lacked the sixth sense physics needs.

He sang while he worked.

In the late 1950s he worked up to 18 hours a day at a small table by the kitchen of a tiny Columbia apartment, and the tone of his singing hinted at his progress.

Proofs by induction were his trademark.

His joke: induction is like high finance. Borrow too little and you have cash-flow problems; borrow too much and you can’t pay the interest.

Long walks and old masters.

He loved walking, sometimes ten miles along Lake Carnegie, and copied old master paintings from his youth.

Legacy

Teaching through lectures, not students.

The Tuesday lectures

At the IAS he lectured on his latest work every Tuesday. Notes from these lectures became books that spread his ideas worldwide.

Collected Papers

His disciple V. S. Varadarajan edited his Collected Papers in four volumes (Springer, 1984).

A bust in Allahabad

In October 1993 a bronze bust was unveiled at a new mathematics and physics institute in Allahabad.

An institute in his name

The Harish-Chandra Research Institute in Prayagraj is named in his honour and works in mathematics and theoretical physics.

On the eve of his sixtieth birthday he doubted whether his work would last. The mathematics that followed has answered him. Roger Howe called him the hero of the heroic era of representation theory.
